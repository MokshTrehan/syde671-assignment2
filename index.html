1.1 · Convolutions from scratch

Convolution method

Convolution flips the kernel in both dimensions and sums the products of overlapping samples. Both implementations use zero padding outside the image. The four-loop implementation iterates over image rows, image columns, kernel rows, and kernel columns. The two-loop implementation keeps the two kernel loops and operates on a complete shifted image slice at each step. Neither implementation uses a convolution routine, FFT, window-view helper, or image-processing library.

For a kernel of height Kh and width Kw, let K denote the kernel size along one axis. The padding is floor(K/2) samples before that axis and floor((K−1)/2) after it. For even kernels, this asymmetry matches SciPy’s “same” output convention. Comparisons use non-symmetric odd and even kernels because a symmetric Gaussian alone cannot reveal a reversed kernel or a one-pixel offset.

Self-portrait results

The self portrait is converted to grayscale using 0.299R + 0.587G + 0.114B, with RGB values in [0, 1]. The working image is 384 × 512 pixels. A normalized 9 × 9 box filter and the derivative kernels Dₓ and Dᵧ are applied separately.

The box filter removes fine texture. Dₓ and Dᵧ emphasize changes across columns and rows, respectively; their signed responses share a symmetric color scale. Zero padding darkens the outer rim of the smoothed image and can produce derivative responses at the frame.

Runtime and numerical agreement

The runtime comparison uses a fixed 96 × 96 image resized from the self portrait. Each method uses float64 values, zero padding, and “same” output. The table reports the median of three calls after one warm-up, with a maximum absolute error of 8.88e-16 across all comparisons.

For an H × W image and a Kh × Kw kernel, both manual methods require O(HWKhKw) arithmetic. Operating on image slices removes Python’s per-pixel loop overhead. The reported times describe performance on this computer.

Boundary conditions

The boundary comparison uses a constant 25 × 25 image with value one and a normalized 9 × 9 box filter. Zero padding gives 25/81 at a corner and 5/9 at the midpoint of an edge; symmetric and periodic extension preserve one everywhere. The “same” setting controls output size, while the boundary setting supplies values outside the image.

Input validation, kernel reversal, and padding

def _convolution_inputs(image, kernel):
    image = np.asarray(image, dtype=np.float64)
    kernel = np.asarray(kernel, dtype=np.float64)
    if image.ndim != 2 or kernel.ndim != 2:
        raise ValueError("image and kernel must both be two dimensional")
    if not image.size or not kernel.size:
        raise ValueError("image and kernel must be nonempty")
    kh, kw = kernel.shape
    # Before = ceil((K-1)/2); after = floor((K-1)/2).
    padded = np.pad(image, ((kh // 2, (kh - 1) // 2),
                            (kw // 2, (kw - 1) // 2)), mode='constant')
    return image, kernel[::-1, ::-1], padded

Four-loop convolution

def convolve_four_loops(image, kernel):
    """True 2-D convolution: two spatial loops and two kernel loops."""
    image, flipped, padded = _convolution_inputs(image, kernel)
    height, width = image.shape
    kh, kw = flipped.shape
    out = np.zeros_like(image)
    for y in range(height):
        for x in range(width):
            for ky in range(kh):
                for kx in range(kw):
                    out[y, x] += flipped[ky, kx] * padded[y + ky, x + kx]
    return out

Two-loop convolution

def convolve_two_loops(image, kernel):
    """True 2-D convolution: kernel loops; spatial products are vectorized."""
    image, flipped, padded = _convolution_inputs(image, kernel)
    height, width = image.shape
    kh, kw = flipped.shape
    out = np.zeros_like(image)
    for ky in range(kh):
        for kx in range(kw):
            out += flipped[ky, kx] * padded[ky:ky + height, kx:kx + width]
    return out

1.2 · Finite difference operator

Gradient calculation

Let I be the grayscale image. Its horizontal and vertical derivative responses are gx = I ∗ [1, 0, −1] and gy = I ∗ [1, 0, −1]ᵀ, where ∗ denotes convolution. Reversing the kernel during convolution makes a left-to-right increase in intensity produce positive gx. The gradient magnitude, √(gx² + gy²), combines the two responses into an unsigned measure of edge strength.

The supplied cameraman image has a thin white border. The crop (1, 7, 535, 541), given as (left, top, right, bottom) with the right and bottom coordinates excluded, removes that border. The remaining square is resized to 256 × 256 pixels with Lanczos interpolation. Calculations use floating-point intensities in [0, 1] without further normalization before thresholding.

A pixel is classified as an edge when its gradient magnitude exceeds the threshold T. A seven-pixel frame is excluded from binary maps and edge-fraction measurements throughout Part 1. This margin excludes responses caused by zero padding and covers the largest Gaussian derivative support used below. The signed derivative and magnitude images show the complete responses.

Threshold comparison

Thresholds of 0.08, 0.16, and 0.24 retain 23.7%, 12.8%, and 9.0% of the evaluated interior, respectively. The selected threshold, 0.16, preserves the person, camera, and tripod more clearly than 0.24 while rejecting much of the ground texture retained at 0.08. Selection is based on this visual balance; detection accuracy is not measured against annotated edges.

1.3 · Derivative of Gaussian

Gaussian derivative kernels

A 13 × 13 Gaussian kernel G with σ = 2 pixels is formed from the outer product of cv2.getGaussianKernel(13, 2) with itself. The image is first smoothed with G, then differentiated with Dₓ and Dᵧ.

Alternatively, Kx = G ∗ Dₓ and Ky = G ∗ Dᵧ combine smoothing and differentiation into one image convolution per direction. These finite-difference derivatives of the sampled Gaussian have supports of 13 × 15 and 15 × 13 pixels because full kernel convolution retains the extra two samples.

Boundary conditions

Convolution associativity gives I ∗ (G ∗ D) = (I ∗ G) ∗ D for an image extended with zeros. Applying “same” output after each stage crops the intermediate blurred image and removes values beyond the original frame. These removed values affect the subsequent derivative near the boundary.

The two successive “same” convolutions differ from a single Gaussian derivative convolution by at most 0.307 over the complete image, while the evaluated interior agrees to 8.95e-16. Retaining the full blurred image, applying the derivative, and cropping once reduces the maximum difference to 8.95e-16. The difference images show where the intermediate crop affects the result.

Edge comparison

The derivative of Gaussian (DoG) suppresses fine grass texture and produces smoother, wider contours than finite differences on the original image. Smoothing can also merge nearby edges and remove weak detail. The selected thresholds are T = 0.08 for DoG and T = 0.16 for the original gradients because smoothing reduces response amplitudes. These thresholds support a visual comparison at separately chosen settings; they do not establish a difference in detection accuracy.

Noise sensitivity

The noise experiment adds Gaussian noise with σ = 0.08 using a fixed random seed, then clips intensities to [0, 1]. Each noisy gradient is compared with the same method applied to the clean image. This comparison measures the change caused by noise while retaining each method’s smoothing effect.

The root mean square (RMS) of the interior vector-gradient residual decreases from 0.15226 for finite differences to 0.01030 for DoG, a factor of 14.8. Gaussian smoothing therefore reduces sensitivity to the added noise in this experiment. The measurement describes this image and noise realization; it does not measure recovery of detail removed by smoothing.

Gradient orientations

Orientation calculation

Gradient orientation is computed from gx and gy using arithmetic operations without calling an arctangent, atan2, or angle routine. First, r = min(|gy|, |gx|)/max(|gy|, |gx|) gives a ratio between zero and one. For r > √2−1, the identity atan(r) = π/4 + atan((r−1)/(r+1)) reduces the magnitude of the series argument to at most √2−1.

The arctangent is approximated with 18 terms of z − z³/3 + z⁵/5 − …, where z is the reduced argument. Coordinate swaps and the signs of the gradient components determine the quadrant. A zero gradient is assigned orientation zero and displayed black.

Color representation and validation

The HSV display maps orientation θ to hue (θ mod 2π)/(2π), with saturation one. Brightness is min(magnitude / p99.5, 1)^0.7, where p99.5 = 0.2386 is the 99.5th percentile of gradient magnitude in the evaluated interior. Stronger gradients appear brighter, while weak background responses remain subdued. The seven-pixel boundary margin is displayed black.

Color indicates the direction of increasing intensity, perpendicular to the edge tangent. Image rows increase downward, so 90° points down. Comparison with NumPy atan2, used only for validation, gives a maximum angular error of 4.44e-16 radians for this image.

Arithmetic-only orientation implementation

def orientation_without_angles(gx, gy, terms=18):
    gx, gy = np.broadcast_arrays(np.asarray(gx, float), np.asarray(gy, float))
    if not np.all(np.isfinite(gx)) or not np.all(np.isfinite(gy)):
        raise ValueError('gradient components must be finite')
    if terms < 1:
        raise ValueError('terms must be positive')
    ax, ay = (np.abs(gx), np.abs(gy))
    high, low = (np.maximum(ax, ay), np.minimum(ax, ay))
    ratio = np.divide(low, high, out=np.zeros_like(high), where=high != 0)
    reduce = ratio > np.sqrt(2.0) - 1.0
    z = np.where(reduce, (ratio - 1.0) / (ratio + 1.0), ratio)
    z2 = z * z
    poly = np.zeros_like(z)
    for n in range(terms - 1, -1, -1):
        poly = (-1.0) ** n / (2 * n + 1) + z2 * poly
    small_angle = z * poly + np.where(reduce, np.pi / 4, 0.0)
    angle = np.where(ay > ax, np.pi / 2 - small_angle, small_angle)
    angle = np.where(gx < 0, np.pi - angle, angle)
    angle = np.where(gy < 0, -angle, angle)
    return np.where(high == 0, 0.0, angle)

2.1 · Image sharpening

Unsharp masking

For an input image I and Gaussian kernel G, let B = G ∗ I be the low-pass image and H = I − B its high-frequency residual. Unsharp masking adds a scaled residual: S = I + αH, where α controls the sharpening amount.

Expanding the residual gives S = (1 + α)I − α(G ∗ I). The equivalent convolution kernel is K = (1 + α)δ − αG, where δ is the unit impulse, so S = K ∗ I requires one convolution. K sums to one and preserves a constant image when the boundary extension also preserves constants.

The experiment uses a 13 × 13 Gaussian with σ = 2 pixels and symmetric boundary extension. Each RGB channel is processed independently in float64, retaining signed residuals and values outside [0, 1] until display export. Symmetric extension avoids the dark borders introduced by zero padding. The Taj Mahal and an original street photograph are processed at α = 0.5, 1, and 2.

Sharpening results

Increasing α strengthens existing edges and texture, but also amplifies noise and halos and increases values outside the display range. Residuals are displayed as 0.5 + 2H, with middle gray representing zero and a fixed amplification of two. No independent normalization is applied to these residuals. The table reports the fraction of scalar RGB values outside [0, 1] before display clipping.

The single-convolution result agrees with S = I + α(I − G ∗ I) at α = 1 to a maximum absolute error of 1.78e-15 across the two images. This comparison checks the equivalence of the residual and kernel formulations.

Recovery after blurring

The recovery experiment first blurs the original street image, then sharpens that blurred image with the same Gaussian at each tested α. Mean squared error (MSE) is measured over RGB values relative to the original image, both before and after display clipping. For the reference range [0, 1], peak signal-to-noise ratio is PSNR = 10 log10(1/MSE), in decibels.

Among the tested settings, α = 2 gives the lowest clipped RGB MSE. The MSE changes from 0.001660 after blurring to 0.001099 after sharpening, while PSNR changes from 27.80 to 29.59 dB. This parameter is selected against the original reference and applies to this comparison.

Sharpening makes blurred transitions steeper but leaves differences from the original image. If G(f) is the Gaussian frequency response, the combined blur and sharpening response is G(f)[1 + α − αG(f)], which generally differs from one. Frequencies strongly attenuated by the blur remain attenuated after this finite amplification, and clipping can remove additional information. The enlarged crop, residual map, and nonzero MSE show that increased edge contrast does not restore all original detail.

2.2 · Hybrid images

Hybrid construction

A hybrid image combines a blurred image with fine detail from a second, aligned image: hybrid = G_sigmaL * A + gain (B - G_sigmaH * B). G_sigma is a Gaussian kernel with standard deviation sigma, and * denotes convolution. A is the human portrait and B is the cat photograph. The three examples are Derek + Nutmeg and two custom portrait/cat pairs.

Image registration

The initial alignment maps both pairs of eye centers to (170,210) and (330,210). Each two-point similarity transform combines rotation, uniform scale and translation. Exact eye alignment leaves the human mouth below the cat’s muzzle in the Derek and astronaut pairs.

For these two pairs, the human image is scaled by 0.85 about the common eye midpoint (250,210) and shifted upward by 8 pixels. Its eye positions become (182,202) and (318,202), while the cat positions remain fixed. This reduces the extra mouth below the muzzle, but the pupils no longer coincide exactly. Obama retains the common eye positions.

Each original is resampled once with the composed similarity transform, using bilinear interpolation and replicated borders. The face proportions are unchanged. The original landmarks and exact 2 x 3 transformation matrices are saved with the experiment records.

Filter settings

A high-pass sigma of 1.5 pixels removes more broad cat shading than sigma 5, so less cat structure remains in the reduced views. The selected low-pass sigma is 8 pixels for Derek and the astronaut, and 6 pixels for Obama. Their respective gains are 2.25, 2.7 and 2.2. These gains retain visible fur detail.

The six filter-width and gain trials keep the selected alignment fixed. More low-pass blur or a broader high-frequency band reduces some double features, but also makes the distant human less clear. The supplementary two-by-two comparisons vary alignment and filtering separately. Settings are selected by visual inspection of these inputs and need not be suitable for other pairs.

Appearance at different scales

The selected astronaut hybrid emphasizes the cat’s fur and eye outlines at 512 pixels. At 64 pixels, the astronaut’s eyes and smile become clearer. The 128- and 256-pixel views retain recognizable features from both sources.

The refined human alignment reduces the extra smile below the cat’s muzzle. A 10% gain reduction from the reference setting limits clipping while retaining fine detail. The final clipped fraction is still slightly higher than for the larger human crop with exactly aligned eyes. The comparison therefore remains a partial change in appearance with scale.

To inspect the effect, view the largest image at normal size and then move farther away, keeping its display size fixed. The reduced versions provide a second comparison. The apparent change depends on screen size, zoom and eyesight, so the pixel-size sequence does not establish a calibrated viewing distance.

Frequency analysis

A Gaussian has no sharp frequency cutoff. Its approximate half-amplitude frequency is sqrt(2 ln 2) / (2 pi sigma) cycles per output pixel. For the selected astronaut pair, this is 0.0234 for the low pass and 0.1249 for the complementary high pass. Filtering uses spatial convolution; the Fourier plots show the resulting frequency content.

Each Fourier spectrum displays log(1 + 1000000 |fftshift(fft2(luminance))| / N), where N is the number of image pixels. Division by N permits comparison of differently sized inputs, and both figures use one shared display maximum. No window or mean subtraction is applied before the Fourier transform.

Numerical representation

Filtering uses reflected boundaries, and filtering and addition retain signed float64 values. Intermediate bands are neither clipped nor contrast-normalized. Display exports clamp values to [0,1] and quantize them to 8 bits; the table reports the fraction of scalar channel values clipped. Supplementary figures include the remaining alignment, component and parameter comparisons, together with the unsuccessful Earth + Moon hybrid.

2.3 · Gaussian and Laplacian stacks

Input preparation

The supplied apple and orange images are burt_apple.png and burt_orange.png. Each 740 × 530 input is resized once to a common working size of 512 × 367 pixels using area interpolation. This reduces the computational cost while preserving the original aspect ratio to the nearest output pixel.

Gaussian and Laplacian decomposition

A Gaussian stack retains the same image dimensions at every level. G0 = I is the original image; G1 through G5 are formed by independently blurring I at σ = 2, 4, 8, 16, and 32 pixels. Each σ therefore describes the smoothing relative to the original image. The stack is constructed explicitly without downsampling or a built-in pyramid routine.

The Laplacian detail bands are Li = Gi − G(i+1) for i = 0, …, 4, and L5 = G5 is the coarse residual. Summing the detail bands gives I − G5; including the residual restores the large-scale brightness and color. The complete sum reconstructs I up to floating-point rounding. The table reports the reconstruction error for each input and for the blended image.

Mask and reconstruction

A binary mask selects apple on the left and orange on the right. Its Gaussian stack uses the same smoothing scales as the image stacks. At level i, Mi is the mask and LAi and LBi are the apple and orange Laplacian bands; the combined band is Si = Mi LAi + (1 − Mi) LBi. This expression also applies to the coarse residual, and summing all Si gives the final image.

The mask has a sharp transition at the finest level and progressively wider transitions at coarser levels. Fine texture and broad changes in brightness and color are therefore blended over different spatial ranges. The contribution figure shows selected detail levels and the complete reconstruction.

2.4 · Multiresolution blending

Comparison and results

The apple and orange are combined using the same inputs and initial selection mask for both methods. A direct binary cut produces a visible vertical change in brightness and color. Multiresolution blending combines corresponding Laplacian bands with Gaussian mask weights, spreading the transition according to scale while retaining local texture.

The mean absolute RGB difference between the columns adjoining the seam decreases from 0.1716 for the binary cut to 0.0279 for the multiresolution blend. This local measurement is consistent with the smoother visible transition. It describes the seam discontinuity rather than overall perceptual quality.

The seam measurement uses the floating-point reconstruction before clipping. Values remain unclipped throughout stack construction and reconstruction, then are limited to [0, 1] for display export. This affects 2.35% of scalar RGB values.

Custom blends with irregular masks

Image pairs and registration

The cat composite places the tabby’s central face within the orange-and-white cat’s ears, outer whiskers and chest. A 24-vertex polygon follows the crown, temples, cheeks and chin, keeping the forehead stripes inside the selection. A similarity transform aligns the eye centers using uniform scale, rotation and translation.

The Earth-Moon composite joins oceans and clouds to lunar craters along an S-shaped boundary. Their similar outlines support this composite, although the same photographs do not produce a clear change of subject in the hybrid experiment.

The largest source contours above luminance 0.04 are fitted with cv2.fitEllipse and mapped affinely to a circle of radius 220 pixels centered at (256,256). This corrects approximately 1% unequal scaling along the Earth image’s two axes; the Moon requires nearly uniform scaling.

Both examples use the complete registered photographs, sampled with bilinear interpolation and replicated borders. Thresholding measures the planetary outlines only; the transformations sample the original RGB pixels. Exposure, color and backgrounds are unchanged, and no retouching or transfer of one background into the other source precedes blending.

Comparison methods

The hard cut, single-scale feathering and multiresolution blend use identical registered arrays and the same underlying binary selection. Feathering applies the exact coarsest Gaussian mask to the full images, so fine details and broad colors mix over the same wide transition. Multiresolution blending applies the corresponding Gaussian mask to each Laplacian band.

Initial mask smoothing

A binary finest mask leaves an abrupt texture change at the cat’s crown and a contrast change where the planetary boundary meets the outer edge. Initial-mask Gaussian widths of 0, 2, 4, 8, 12 and 16 pixels are compared for each example. Sigma 8 softens these joins while keeping the tabby’s eyes, nose and muzzle inside the selection; larger widths mix fine texture over a wider region.

Only the selection weights change in this comparison; the source photographs and image detail bands remain fixed. The selected mask widths are nominally 8, 8.2, 8.9, 11.3, 17.9 and 33.0 pixels for the cat, with 64.5 pixels also used for Earth-Moon. The oraple retains a binary finest mask, and the supplementary figures show the crown and lower-edge comparisons.

Number of detail bands

The cat blend uses five detail bands with sigma = 2, 4, 8, 16 and 32 pixels. The Earth-Moon blend uses six, adding sigma = 64 pixels, and both include the coarse residual. Supplementary comparisons test four, five and six bands while keeping all other inputs fixed.

For the cat, a maximum sigma of 16 pixels leaves a more visible facial patch. Sigma 32 softens the coat boundary, while sigma 64 spreads the color transition farther into the surrounding fur. The Earth-Moon blend uses sigma 64 because the brightness mismatch extends across a large part of the disk.

Boundary appearance

The direct cut leaves a visible polygon boundary around the cat’s face. Multiresolution blending reduces the coat-color change and retains more distinct tabby detail around the cheek and muzzle than broad feathering, which mixes both faces over a wider region. Clouds and craters also remain more localized at the Earth-Moon boundary. These observations concern the visible transitions in these two examples.

The boundary-jump measurement is the mean absolute RGB difference between adjacent pixels on opposite sides of the original binary contour. It includes natural image texture as well as the seam. Feathering gives smaller values because it mixes more detail, so a smaller jump alone does not establish a better-looking or physically realistic composite.

Color comparison

RGB is used for the final composites. The tabby’s coat color separates its face from the white fur and orange ears, while blue oceans distinguish Earth from the nearly neutral Moon. These regions are less distinct in grayscale, although the boundary is still smoothed.

Limitations

Multiresolution blending does not correct differences in anatomy or surface structure. The cats retain differences in muzzle shape and fur direction, and lunar features do not continue Earth’s clouds. Smoothing the finest mask reduces the abrupt outer-edge join, but the Earth-Moon composite remains an imaginary surface.

Processing retains signed float64 values. Only the final display images are clipped to [0,1].

Summary and observations

The main lesson from these experiments is that spatial scale determines which image structures are preserved. Smoothing reduces fine variation before differentiation. Hybrid images combine the low-frequency structure of one source with high-frequency detail from another. Multiresolution blending uses narrow transitions for fine detail and wider transitions for brightness and color differences.

The numerical tests establish consistency of filtering and reconstruction. Visual comparisons also show that these operations cannot fully resolve differences in facial geometry or source texture. Image registration and parameter selection therefore remain important even when reconstruction errors are close to floating-point precision.
